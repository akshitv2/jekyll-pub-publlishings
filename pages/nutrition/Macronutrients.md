# Macronutrients

Nutrients the human body requires in large amounts to provide energy, support growth, and maintain metabolic function.
Essentially 4:

1. Carbohydrates
2. Proteins
3. Fats
4. Water

1. ## Carbohydrates
    - Body's primary and most accessible energy source
    - Break down into glucose, which fuels cellular function, brain activity, and physical exertion.
    - **Energy Yield**: 4 calories per gram.
    - Types:
        - Simple: Fast-digesting sugars found in fruits, milk, and refined sweets.
        - Complex: Slow-digesting starches and fiber found in whole grains, legumes, and vegetables.
2. ## Proteins
    - Structural building blocks of the body.
    - Made up of 20 amino acids, 9 of which are "essential,"
        - i.e. the body cannot synthesize them and must get them from food.
    - **Energy Yield**: 4 calories per gram.
    - Detailed Breakdown:
        - **Essential Amino Acids (EAAs)**:
            - 9 amino acids that the body cannot manufacture. They must be obtained through
              diet.
            - The 9 EAAs: Histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and
              valine.

3. ## Fats
    - Fats are dense energy sources essential for cellular health, organ protection, and nutrient absorption.
    - **Energy Yield**: 9 calories per gram.
    - Types:
        - Unsaturated: Healthy fats (monounsaturated and polyunsaturated) found in olive oil, avocados, nuts, and fatty
          fish.
        - Saturated: Found mostly in animal products and tropical oils; should be consumed in moderation.
        - Trans Fats: Artificial fats found in processed foods; should be avoided.
    - Key Functions: Absorbing fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), maintaining cell membrane integrity, insulating
      organs, and regulating hormone production.
    - Detailed Breakdown:
        1. Monounsaturated Fatty Acids (MUFAs):
            - Chemical Structure: Contain one double bond in the carbon chain, leaving fewer hydrogen atoms. They are
              typically liquid at room temperature but turn solid when chilled.
            - Physiological Impact: Highly stable, cardioprotective, and anti-inflammatory. They lower low-density
              lipoprotein (LDL/"bad") cholesterol while maintaining high-density lipoprotein (HDL/"good") cholesterol.
            - Primary Sources:
                - Olive oil (specifically extra virgin)
                - Avocados and avocado oil
                - Nuts (macadamias, almonds, cashews, pecans)
        2. Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFAs)
            - Chemical Structure: Contain two or more double bonds in the carbon chain. They remain liquid at both room
              temperature and cold temperatures. They are highly reactive and prone to oxidation (rancidity) when
              exposed to heat, light, or oxygen.
            - Subcategories (The Essential Fatty Acids): Humans lack the enzymes to insert double bonds at certain
              positions, making two specific PUFAs strictly essential:
                - Omega-3 Fatty Acids (Alpha-Linolenic Acid / ALA): Reduces systemic inflammation, supports brain
                  structure, and prevents blood clotting.
                    - Marine Sources (Highly bioavailable EPA/DHA): Salmon, mackerel, sardines, anchovies, krill oil.
                    - Plant Sources (ALA, requires conversion to EPA/DHA): Chia seeds, flaxseeds, walnuts, hemp seeds.
                - Omega-6 Fatty Acids (Linoleic Acid / LA): Involved in cellular signaling and inflammatory responses
                  required for immune defense and tissue repair.
                    - Sources: Corn oil, soybean oil, sunflower oil, poultry fat.
                - The Ratio Factor: While essential, modern diets often feature an excessive Omega-6 to Omega-3 ratio (
                  e.g., 16:1 instead of an optimal 4:1 or 2:1), which can shift the body into a pro-inflammatory state.
        3. Saturated Fatty Acids (SFAs)
            - Chemical Structure: Contain zero double bonds. The carbon chain is completely "saturated" with hydrogen
              atoms. This rigid, straight structure allows them to pack tightly together, making them solid at room
              temperature.
            - Physiological Impact: Highly stable and resistant to heat damage (making them safe for high-heat cooking).
              They raise total cholesterol (both HDL and LDL). Current metabolic consensus advises limiting intake to
              under 10% of total daily calories, though impacts vary based on the specific food matrix (e.g., fermented
              dairy vs. processed meats).
            - Primary Sources:
                - Animal products (fatty cuts of beef, pork, lamb, poultry skin)
                - Dairy (butter, ghee, heavy cream, whole milk, cheese)
                    - Tropical oils (coconut oil, palm oil, cocoa butter)
        4. Trans Fatty Acids (Trans Fats)
            - Chemical Structure: Unsaturated fats that undergo a chemical process called partial hydrogenation, turning
              liquid oils into solid fats to prolong shelf life. This flips the hydrogen atoms into a "trans"
              configuration.
            - Physiological Impact: Highly toxic to metabolic health. They aggressively raise LDL cholesterol, lower HDL
              cholesterol, induce endothelial dysfunction, and trigger systemic inflammation.
            - Primary Sources: Industrial fried foods, commercial baked goods, shortening, and margarine. (Note: Small,
              benign amounts of naturally occurring trans fats, like vaccenic acid, are found in ruminant meat and
              dairy).
    - Daily Intake:
        - Total Fat Baseline: 20% to 35% of total daily caloric intake (per US Dietary Guidelines and WHO). Dropping
          below 20% can impair hormone production and cause fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies.
